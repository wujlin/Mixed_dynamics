\begin{abstract}
Sudden polarization and surges of collective activity can emerge on social platforms even without commensurate external shocks. We propose that such emotional collapses reflect a loss of resilience in a mixed information ecology, where a stabilizing mainstream channel competes with an amplifying user-generated channel. We formalize this competition in a mixed-feedback model that separates polarization direction from intensity, controlled by a single parameter that shifts channel balance. The theory predicts a transition from a stable neutral state to self-sustaining polarized states, together with critical slowing down that provides an early-warning signal before the threshold is crossed. Agent-based simulations on networks reproduce the predicted transition, the dynamical precursor, and the sensitivity of the transition boundary across topologies and structural parameters. In large-scale online discussions, we observe statistical patterns consistent with the mechanism: higher activity is associated with larger polarization jumps, and greater user-generated dominance is associated with higher polarization volatility. These results suggest that managing channel balance can be as important as moderating content for restoring collective emotional resilience.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} collective emotion, phase transitions, mixed feedback, critical slowing down, media ecology

\section{Introduction}
Collective emotions can remain stable for long periods and then shift abruptly, producing rapid polarization and surges of activity. Online platforms amplify this volatility: nonlinear feedback, social influence, and adaptive exposure can turn small fluctuations into system-wide regime changes \citep{Centola2010,Watts2002,Becker2017}. A central challenge is therefore to identify structural conditions under which a seemingly stable emotional climate becomes fragile and prone to abrupt transitions.

Classic models of collective behavior capture how nonlinear influence can create tipping points. Threshold and cascade models \citep{Granovetter1978,Watts2002} and network-based influence experiments \citep{Centola2010,Becker2017} show that small changes in reinforcement can trigger large-scale coordination. Yet many formal approaches represent collective affect with a single axis, making it difficult to separate who dominates from how heated the system is. More detailed agent-based and data-driven models can reproduce rich dynamics \citep{Helfmann2023}, but often lack an analytic control parameter that delineates regime boundaries and enables mechanism-based predictions.

These modeling challenges are amplified in online contexts, where the information environment itself is endogenously shaped by a mixture of sources and platform-mediated attention. Empirical studies of algorithmic exposure and echo chambers document reinforcement and segregation in online information flows \citep{Bakshy2015,DelVicario2016SciRep,DelVicario2016PNAS}. Related work on information cocoons highlights how user-generated signals can become self-reinforcing under adaptive dynamics \citep{Piao2023}. These perspectives motivate feedback-based mechanisms, but most models still treat media influence as a single aggregate channel rather than an explicit competition between stabilizing and amplifying forces whose balance can shift over time.

The COVID-19 pandemic illustrates this mixed ecology, when mainstream channels often attempted to dampen panic while user-generated channels amplified high-arousal narratives \citep{Green2020,Bavel2020,Wang2022,Aslam2020,Chou2020,Liu2024}. Risk amplification frameworks \citep{Kasperson1988} and studies of moral outrage and emotional contagion \citep{Goldenberg2021,Brady2023,Crockett2017,Kramer2014,Fan2014} describe relevant mechanisms, yet they are largely descriptive and do not specify when mixed influence pushes a system across a stability threshold. Without parameterizing channel balance, it remains hard to explain why similar populations can exhibit sharply different levels of polarization volatility and abrupt shifts.

Here we develop a minimal mixed-feedback model in which a stabilizing mainstream channel and an amplifying user-generated channel compete. We parameterize the balance with a single control parameter $r\in[0,1]$ ($r=0$ mainstream-dominant; $r=1$ user-generated-dominant), allowing us to ask how structural shifts in media ecology reshape collective resilience. A key feature of the framework is the separation of polarization direction from intensity: we introduce a direction variable $q$ (net emotional imbalance) and an activity variable $a$ (overall engagement, or depletion of moderates). This direction--intensity decomposition links the mechanism to observables and clarifies why elevated activity can act as a precursor rather than merely a consequence.

This framework yields testable predictions across multiple scales. At the mean-field level, the theory predicts an analytic critical point and an effective potential describing a transition from a stable neutral state to bistable polarized states. Near this boundary, the model predicts critical slowing down---a dynamical early-warning signal as recovery becomes progressively slower before the transition. When we allow the user-generated channel to respond to overall activity (a realistic coupling), the transition structure changes qualitatively: direction and intensity become entangled, producing a broader crossover in which activity rises early as the system approaches fragility. We validate these predictions in network simulations across topologies and systematically map how psychological thresholds, information density, media ecology, and local coupling shift the transition boundary.

Finally, we confront the theoretical signatures with large-scale online discussions by constructing empirical proxies for polarization, activity, and media composition and testing whether the predicted statistical associations appear in real data. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} summarizes this workflow, connecting the mechanism, the simulation validation, and the empirical signatures.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\noindent Our strategy is theory-driven. We first build a minimal mechanistic model that captures the competition between stabilizing and amplifying feedback channels. From this model, we derive analytic predictions for the bifurcation and its dynamical precursors. We then test robustness in network simulations before confronting these predictions with empirical data. This sequence ensures that our statistical tests are grounded in well-defined theoretical signatures rather than exploratory pattern-finding.

\subsection{Model framework}
We consider a population of $N$ individuals with three arousal states: high ($H$), medium ($M$), and low ($L$). Let $(\rho_H,\rho_M,\rho_L)$ denote the population fractions with $\rho_H+\rho_M+\rho_L=1$. To separate polarization direction from intensity, we introduce two macroscopic variables: the polarization direction
\begin{equation}
q(t)=\rho_H(t)-\rho_L(t),
\label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
and the system activity
\begin{equation}
a(t)=\rho_H(t)+\rho_L(t)=1-\rho_M(t).
\label{eq:a}
\end{equation}
Here, $q(t)$ measures which side dominates and $a(t)$ measures how heated the system is. These variables decouple symmetry breaking from the depletion of the moderate buffer $\rho_M=1-a$, with $\rho_H=(a+q)/2$ and $\rho_L=(a-q)/2$. When the buffer collapses ($a\to 1$), the dynamics effectively reduce to competition between extreme states.

At each update, an individual samples $k$ risk signals from the environment, each labeled ``risk'' with probability $p_i$. Let $S\sim\text{Binomial}(k,p_i)$ denote the number of risk signals and $\hat{p}=S/k$ the perceived risk fraction. Individuals transition to $H$ if $\hat{p}\ge\phi$, to $L$ if $\hat{p}\le\theta$, and otherwise remain in $M$, where $\theta<\phi$ define low and high arousal thresholds.

The information environment is modeled as a competition between a stabilizing mainstream channel (negative feedback) and an amplifying user-generated channel (positive feedback). The mainstream signal is
\begin{equation}
p^{\text{main}}(q)=\frac{1-q}{2},
\label{eq:pmain}
\end{equation}
which counteracts the current polarization.

The user-generated channel, unlike mainstream media, can respond to the current collective state in multiple ways. To separate analytic tractability from empirical realism, we use two complementary formulations. The symmetric validation regime enforces directional symmetry ($q\to -q$) and allows closed-form derivation of the critical point. The asymmetric, realistic regime allows amplification to depend on activity as well as direction, capturing attention-driven reinforcement observed on real platforms.

In the symmetric validation regime, used for analytical verification, we set
\begin{equation}
p^{\text{we}}(q)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{q}{2},
\label{eq:pwe_sym}
\end{equation}
which enforces $q\to -q$ symmetry. In the asymmetric, realistic regime we allow activity coupling via
\begin{equation}
p^{\text{we}}(q,a)=\rho_H=\frac{a+q}{2}.
\label{eq:pwe_asym}
\end{equation}
The overall environmental risk is the weighted average
\begin{equation}
p_{\text{env}}(q,a;r)=\frac{(1-r)n_m p^{\text{main}} + r n_w p^{\text{we}}}{(1-r)n_m + r n_w}, \qquad r\in[0,1],
\label{eq:penv}
\end{equation}
where $n_m$ and $n_w$ are the baseline strengths of the mainstream and user-generated channels, and the control parameter $r$ tunes their relative weight ($r=0$: mainstream-dominant; $r=1$: user-generated-dominant).

\subsection{Mean-field analysis and critical point}
We next derive the mean-field conditions under which polarization becomes self-sustaining in the symmetric validation regime. Under this regime, $q=0$ is a fixed point with $p_{\text{env}}=1/2$. Linearizing the mean-field dynamics around $q=0$ yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{dq}{dt}=-q+\mathcal{S}(p),
\label{eq:q_dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}(p)$ is the collective response to the environmental risk $p=p_{\text{env}}$. We define the psychological sensitivity
\begin{equation}
\chi=\left.\frac{d\mathcal{S}}{dp}\right|_{p=1/2},
\label{eq:chi}
\end{equation}
where $\chi$ measures how strongly the collective response changes with the information environment near neutrality ($p=1/2$). We also define the feedback gradient $\Gamma=\left.\partial p_{\text{env}}/\partial q\right|_{q=0}$. Near neutrality, $\mathcal{S}(p)\approx \chi(p-1/2)$ and $p_{\text{env}}\approx 1/2+\Gamma q$, so Eq.~(\ref{eq:q_dynamics}) reduces to $dq/dt\approx(-1+\chi\Gamma)q$ (Supplementary Materials S2). The neutral fixed point loses stability when the restoring coefficient changes sign, yielding $\chi\Gamma=1$ and the analytic critical point
\begin{equation}
r_c=\frac{n_m(\chi+2)}{n_m(\chi+2)+n_w(\chi-2)}.
\label{eq:rc}
\end{equation}
Near $r\approx r_c$, the order parameter obeys a Ginzburg-Landau form,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dq}{dt}=-\frac{\partial V_{\text{eff}}(q)}{\partial q}+\eta(t),\qquad V_{\text{eff}}(q)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha q^2+\frac{u}{4}q^4,
\label{eq:gl}
\end{equation}
where $\eta(t)$ represents stochastic fluctuations due to finite-population effects, modeled as white noise with variance scaling as $1/N$ (Supplementary Materials S2). With $\alpha\propto(r_c-r)$ and $u>0$, this predicts a pitchfork bifurcation and critical slowing down, $\tau\sim 1/|r_c-r|$.

These predictions---bifurcation, bistability, and critical slowing down---are derived under idealized mean-field assumptions; we next test their robustness under networked agent-based dynamics.

\subsection{Agent-based network simulations}
Agent-based simulations serve two purposes. First, they test whether the mean-field signatures (bifurcation, bistability, and critical slowing down) persist under network structure and finite-size fluctuations. Second, they map how structural parameters---psychological thresholds, information density, media ecology, and local coupling---shift the effective transition boundary.

We implement microscopic dynamics on Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi (ER) and Barab\'asi--Albert (BA) networks with size $N$ and average degree $\langle k\rangle$. Updates are asynchronous: each step updates a fraction $f$ of nodes. We report time in steps and in sweeps, where one sweep corresponds to $N$ asynchronous updates (approximately $1/f$ steps). To capture local reinforcement, we introduce a coupling parameter $\beta$ that blends the global environment $p_{\text{env}}$ with neighbor information when forming each agent's perceived risk probability; $\beta=0$ recovers the well-mixed limit. Here $\langle k\rangle$ denotes average network degree, whereas $k$ denotes the number of sampled signals per update.

From node states we compute polarization $Q$ and activity $a$ using the same definitions as in Eqs.~(\ref{eq:q})--(\ref{eq:a}). In the symmetric regime, trajectories select either polarized branch with (approximately) equal probability, so the signed mean $\langle Q\rangle$ cancels by symmetry. We therefore track $|Q|$ or align trajectory signs across realizations before averaging.

Finite-size effects blur sharp transitions, so critical-point estimation can be sensitive to noise. We therefore estimate transition points using Binder cumulant crossings, which are more robust than susceptibility peaks:
\begin{equation}
U_4(r;N)=1-\frac{\langle Q^4\rangle}{3\langle Q^2\rangle^2}.
\label{eq:binder}
\end{equation}

To quantify critical slowing down, we estimate relaxation and autocorrelation indicators near the transition. We estimate the relaxation time $\tau$ in the deterministic mean-field dynamics by initializing at a small perturbation and measuring the time required for $|q|$ to decay by a factor of $e$. For stochastic trajectories (SDE and ABM), we compute short-lag autocorrelation of the polarization time series ($Q$) over steady-state windows and report autocorrelation at lag 1 sweep (after converting ABM steps to sweeps as above).

To characterize which structural conditions promote fragility, we map how parameters move the transition boundary. First, we compute the analytic sensitivity $\chi(\phi,\theta,k)$ on a dense grid $\phi,\theta\in[0.1,0.9]$ (step 0.01) and evaluate $r_c$ via Eq.~(\ref{eq:rc}) wherever a transition exists ($\chi>2$). Second, to validate these predictions and assess finite-size corrections, we conduct simulation-based sweeps around the baseline $(\phi,\theta)=(0.54,0.46)$. We vary information density $k\in\{10,20,50,100,200,500\}$, media ecology ratio $n_w/n_m\in[0.1,2.0]$ (40 values), and local coupling $\beta\in\{0,0.02,0.05,0.1,0.2\}$. For each parameter combination, we sample $r$ on a 201-point grid in $[0,1]$ and estimate $r_c$ as the value at which the median $|Q|(r)$ across seeds has maximal slope. We obtain 95\% confidence intervals by bootstrap resampling seeds.

\subsection{Empirical data and operational proxies}
Mean-field theory and network simulations predict two empirical signatures. First, elevated activity should be associated with larger polarization jumps. Second, greater user-generated dominance should be associated with higher polarization volatility. Testing these predictions in real-world data requires mapping the model's latent variables to observable proxies. We describe the data sources and proxy construction below.

We use a Weibo corpus spanning January 2019 to July 2025, including the early COVID-19 period. The corpus includes posts from mainstream sources, government accounts, user-generated media accounts, and public users. After de-duplication and filtering, it contains 880,100 unique posts; the empirical analyses reported here use an annotated subset of 90,982 posts. Each post is annotated for emotional arousal (High/Medium/Low) and risk content (risk/norisk) using \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3-8B} via a vLLM-based OpenAI-compatible API with structured prompts and a fixed JSON schema (temperature=0.1, max\_tokens=512; prompt and schema in Supplementary Materials S1). On a manually rated subset ($n=3{,}000$), the LLM annotations achieved 83\% overall accuracy (exact-match agreement) with human raters (Supplementary Materials S1).

We aggregate posts into time windows and compute window-level proxies. We use two aggregation scales to balance temporal resolution against sampling density. The primary analysis uses 4-hour (4H) windows. We additionally evaluate the media-dominance--volatility signature under 12-hour (12H) aggregation (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp:h2-density-12h}), which increases per-window sample size at the cost of temporal granularity and yields more stable within-segment estimates of $r_{\text{proxy}}$ and volatility. Results are reported for the 4H primary analysis unless otherwise noted. Within each window, we compute arousal fractions $(X_H,X_M,X_L)$ from public-user posts; windows with fewer than 5 public posts are treated as missing. We compute media-type counts from all posts.

From these windows we construct polarization $Q=X_H-X_L$ and activity $a=X_H+X_L$, corresponding to net imbalance and total high-arousal fraction in the model. Media composition is proxied by
\begin{equation}
r_{\text{proxy}}=\frac{n_{\text{wemedia}}}{n_{\text{wemedia}}+n_{\text{mainstream}}+n_{\text{government}}},
\label{eq:rproxy}
\end{equation}
treating government accounts as part of the mainstream channel because they typically share the stabilizing orientation of official narratives during public crises.

For segment-level tests, we group consecutive windows into weekly segments (42 windows under 4H aggregation; 14 under 12H). Weekly segments provide enough within-segment points for derivative-based statistics while maintaining stable segment summaries. We retain segments with sufficient coverage: at least 10 valid windows for $Q$ and $a$, and at least 5 valid consecutive differences for the derivative-based jump statistic. Within each retained segment, volatility is $\text{std}(Q)$ and jump intensity is $\text{jump}_{q95}$, the 95th percentile of $|d|Q|/dt|$ computed on consecutive windows (i.e., excluding gaps); these quantities are used in the correlation analyses reported in Results.

\section{Results}
\noindent The results follow a theory-to-data evidence chain linking channel balance to collective resilience. We derive the direction--intensity bifurcation and its potential-landscape interpretation, validate these signatures in network simulations, quantify dynamical and structural precursors (critical slowing down and parameter-dependent boundary shifts), and test corresponding patterns in empirical data.

\subsection{Direction--intensity bifurcation}
Collective polarization is often attributed to external shocks, but competing media feedback can also generate endogenous instability when channel balance shifts. We characterize this mechanism by tracking two macroscopic variables: polarization direction $q$ (which side dominates) and activity $a$ (how heated the system is, i.e., depletion of the moderate buffer $\rho_M=1-a$). This separation matters because $a$ controls how much ``moderate mass'' remains to absorb perturbations: as $a\to 1$ the dynamics effectively reduce to two-state competition, increasing susceptibility to abrupt direction changes.

Mean-field analysis confirms this mechanism. In the symmetric validation regime, tuning the control parameter $r$ produces a sharp pitchfork bifurcation in $q$ at the analytic threshold $r_c$. For the baseline configuration $n_m=10$, $n_w=5$, $\phi=0.54$, $\theta=0.46$, and $k=50$, the theory predicts $r_c\approx 0.753$. The effective potential $V_{\text{eff}}(q)$ shifts from a single well to a double well as the stabilizing mainstream channel is down-weighted, yielding bistability and branch selection---a mechanistic picture of polarized ``emotional bubbles'' as a loss of resilience (Fig.~\ref{fig:theory}a,b).

However, this clean bifurcation relies on a simplifying assumption: the user-generated channel responds symmetrically to positive and negative polarization. In realistic settings, attention-driven platforms tend to amplify whichever direction is already dominant, breaking this symmetry. To assess robustness, we compare the symmetric validation regime with a realistic activity-coupled asymmetric regime (Fig.~\ref{fig:theory}c).

The comparison reveals a qualitative difference. Under symmetry, $|q|$ stays near zero until close to $r_c$ and $a$ rises mainly near the transition. Under activity-coupled asymmetry, direction and intensity become entangled: both $|q|$ and $a$ begin to increase already for $r<r_c$, turning the sharp tipping point into a broader crossover.

This direction--intensity entanglement has a crucial implication: elevated activity is not merely a symptom but a structural precursor. As stabilizing feedback weakens, the moderate buffer depletes (rising $a$), bringing the system closer to criticality even before a polarized direction is selected. Consistent with this, in asymmetric simulations activity relaxes faster than polarization ($\tau_a/\tau_q\approx 0.70$; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp:tau-ratio}), indicating that $a$ must be tracked jointly with $q$. We next test whether these mean-field signatures persist under finite-size fluctuations and network topology.

\subsection{Network validation}
The bifurcation mechanism derived above rests on mean-field assumptions---infinite population and well-mixed interactions---that real social systems violate. If the transition were merely an artifact of this idealization, the framework would have limited predictive value. Network topology and finite-size fluctuations can blur, shift, or even suppress collective transitions, so we test whether the predicted signatures---pitchfork bifurcation, bistability, and direction--intensity coupling---survive under realistic networked dynamics.

Agent-based simulations on Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi (ER) and Barab\'asi--Albert (BA) networks confirm that the bifurcation persists beyond mean-field. In the symmetric regime, trajectories select polarized branches with equal probability, so the signed mean $\langle Q\rangle$ cancels by symmetry; tracking $|Q|$ or aligning branch signs recovers the expected pitchfork transition (Fig.~\ref{fig:network_validation}a). Finite-size scaling provides further support: Binder cumulant curves $U_4(r;N)$ for multiple system sizes cross near the theoretical $r_c$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:network_validation}b), indicating that the estimated critical point is weakly affected by finite-size corrections within our resolution. This crossing behavior is more robust than susceptibility peaks, which converge more slowly with system size (Supplementary Figs.~\ref{fig:supp:signed-vs-abs-q}--\ref{fig:supp:susceptibility-fss}).

Beyond the symmetric regime, theory predicts that activity-coupled asymmetry qualitatively reshapes the transition: rather than a sharp tipping point, polarization $|Q|$ and activity $a$ should rise together as user-generated dominance increases. We test this prediction by tracking steady-state $a(r)$ alongside $|Q|$ in network simulations. Under symmetry, activity rises mainly near $r_c$; under activity-coupled asymmetry it increases earlier, consistent with direction--intensity coupling (Fig.~\ref{fig:network_validation}c). This coupling motivates the empirical analysis: if activity acts as a structural precursor, segments with elevated activity should exhibit larger polarization jumps (Section~3.5).

Taken together, network simulations show that the phase-transition mechanism is structurally robust rather than a mean-field artifact: the bifurcation persists under both ER and BA topologies, the critical point remains close to the analytic estimate, and activity-coupled asymmetry induces direction--intensity coupling instead of eliminating the transition. More broadly, network-mediated threshold dynamics often retain collective transitions while topology modulates their sharpness and location \citep{Watts2002,Centola2010,Centola2018Tipping,Iacopini2019}.

\subsection{Critical slowing down}
The bifurcation analysis tells us when polarization becomes self-sustaining, but it is intrinsically retrospective: the transition is observed only after the system has crossed the threshold. For intervention, a dynamical precursor detectable before the crossing would be more valuable. Mean-field theory predicts such a leading indicator: as $r$ approaches $r_c$ from below, the restoring force weakens and recovery from perturbations becomes progressively slower. This phenomenon, known as critical slowing down, is a canonical early-warning signal for critical transitions across complex systems \citep{Scheffer2009,Scheffer2012,Dakos2012,Lenton2012RSTA}. Here we test whether the same precursor arises in our mixed-feedback framework and persists under stochastic networked dynamics.

Mean-field dynamics confirm the predicted scaling. In the deterministic regime, perturbations around the stable fixed point relax exponentially with a time constant that diverges as $\tau\propto (r_c-r)^{-1}$ for $r<r_c$; a log--log fit yields an exponent close to one (Fig.~\ref{fig:csd}a). Mechanistically, this divergence reflects the vanishing curvature of the effective potential: as the single well $V_{\mathrm{eff}}(q)$ flattens before splitting into a double well, small perturbations decay increasingly slowly.

Agent-based simulations reproduce this slowing under network structure and finite-size fluctuations. After aligning discrete ABM updates with continuous-time theory by converting steps to sweeps (Methods), we evaluate short-lag autocorrelation as $r$ varies. Both the reduced SDE and the full ABM show rising autocorrelation as $r\to r_c$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:csd}b), indicating increasingly persistent fluctuations near the boundary. Additional early-warning diagnostics, including variance trends and multi-lag autocorrelation checks, are consistent with this precursor (Supplementary Figs.~\ref{fig:supp:ews-ac-var}--\ref{fig:supp:abm-multilag-ac}).

Representative trajectories provide an intuitive view: far from criticality the system relaxes within a few sweeps, whereas near-critical systems exhibit prolonged fluctuations and slower recovery (Fig.~\ref{fig:csd}c). Together, these results show that critical slowing down complements the static bifurcation picture by providing a leading indicator detectable before the transition. Translating this signal to empirical social-media time series, however, requires sufficiently dense and approximately stationary observations; uneven sampling and strong external forcing can mimic rising autocorrelation or variance. We return to these constraints when confronting the predicted early-warning signature with data in Section~3.5.

\subsection{Parameter landscape}
The bifurcation mechanism above is defined around a single baseline configuration; for practical prediction, we need to understand which systems are most vulnerable and what structural shifts can push a stable system toward criticality. The critical boundary $r_c$ is not universal. We map how it depends on four structural factors: psychological thresholds $(\phi,\theta)$, information density $k$, media ecology $n_w/n_m$, and local network coupling $\beta$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}).

Among these factors, media ecology is the most directly actionable. Increasing user-generated dominance (larger $n_w/n_m$) systematically lowers $r_c$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}c): when amplifying user-generated feedback outweighs stabilizing mainstream feedback, a smaller shift in the control parameter $r$ suffices to destabilize the neutral state. This mechanism aligns with empirical accounts of amplification and cocooning in online information environments \citep{DelVicario2016SciRep,Piao2023}.

Psychological thresholds and information density shape vulnerability through the response sensitivity $\chi$. A transition exists only when $\chi>2$, defining a feasible region in the $(\phi,\theta)$ plane (Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}a), with the baseline parameters lying inside this vulnerable zone. Increasing $k$ sharpens thresholded perception and typically amplifies responsiveness, but the induced shift in $r_c$ is non-monotonic (Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}b), reflecting competing effects of enhanced sensitivity and reduced finite-sample variability in finite systems.

Local coupling $\beta$ captures neighborhood reinforcement beyond the global environment. As $\beta$ increases, local reinforcement shifts the macroscopic boundary (Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}d) and can bias branch selection, consistent with the general role of local reinforcement in threshold-based contagion \citep{Granovetter1978,Centola2010,Iacopini2019}. These parameter effects are robust across diagnostic checks: symmetry tests and alternative order-parameter definitions prevent branch-cancellation artifacts, finite-size extrapolation resolves the deviation at $k=500$, and additional diagnostics quantify $\beta$-induced bias and the $k$-dependence decomposition (Supplementary Figs.~\ref{fig:supp:symmetric-diagonal}--\ref{fig:supp:beta-q-a}).

Taken together, the landscape identifies structural fragility conditions: high sensitivity (large $\chi$), intermediate information density, user-generated dominance, and stronger local reinforcement all move the system closer to criticality. Because media ecology and local coupling can, in principle, be influenced by platform design and exposure mixing, the landscape suggests structural intervention levers complementary to content-level moderation. The media-ecology effect motivates our empirical proxy $r_{\text{proxy}}$: if shifts toward user-generated dominance lower resilience in the model, they should manifest as increased volatility in real-world data.

\subsection{Empirical signatures}
Having established the theoretical mechanism and validated its robustness in simulations, we now test whether its statistical signatures appear in real-world social-media data. We focus on two segment-level associations implied by the direction--intensity decomposition. First, if activity reflects depletion of the moderate buffer, more active segments should exhibit larger polarization jumps. Second, if media ecology shifts the effective stability boundary, segments with greater user-generated dominance should exhibit higher polarization volatility. Because sampling density and missingness vary over time, we aggregate window-level proxies into weekly segments and use density-aware diagnostics (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp:time-series-overview}).

Consistent with the precursor hypothesis, in the pooled dataset segments with higher mean activity show larger jump intensity (95th percentile of $|d|Q|/dt|$; Pearson $r=0.241$, $p=0.00798$; Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical}a). This pattern supports the mechanism that moderate-buffer depletion increases susceptibility to abrupt direction changes.

For the media-ecology signature, reliable estimation of $r_{\text{proxy}}$ and volatility requires sufficient within-window media counts; we therefore evaluate this association using 12-hour aggregation in the high-density subset. User-generated dominance is positively associated with polarization volatility (Pearson $r=0.429$, $p=6.03\times 10^{-7}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical}b). The association remains after controlling for segment sampling density (partial $r=0.386$, $p=8.79\times 10^{-6}$; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp:h2-density-12h}), consistent with the parameter-landscape prediction that stronger user-generated amplification lowers resilience.

Taken together, the empirical tests provide targeted support for the mixed-feedback framework. The activity--jump association links moderate-buffer depletion to heightened susceptibility, while the media-dominance--volatility association links shifts in channel balance to reduced resilience. Supplementary Figs.~\ref{fig:supp:scatter-h1-h2}--\ref{fig:supp:eventstudy-h4} report additional diagnostics across datasets and event-aligned early-warning analyses under realistic missingness.

\section{Discussion}
This study develops a mixed-feedback theory of collective emotion that links competing media dynamics to phase-transition behavior. By combining thresholded arousal updating with antagonistic feedback channels, the model yields an analytic critical point $r_c$ and an effective potential that interprets emotional bubbles as a loss of stabilizing feedback that creates bistability. Beyond static bifurcation, the theory predicts critical slowing down as a dynamical signature near instability. Agent-based simulations reproduce these signatures under networked interactions, diagnose finite-size effects with Binder cumulants, and map how thresholds, information density, media ecology, and local coupling shift the effective transition boundary.

Empirical analyses provide targeted support. In the pooled dataset, higher activity predicts larger jump intensity, consistent with the mechanism that depletion of moderates increases susceptibility to abrupt shifts. In the high-density subset, greater user-generated dominance (higher $r_{\text{proxy}}$) is associated with higher volatility and remains significant after controlling for segment sampling density under 12-hour aggregation. This pattern aligns with the parameter-landscape prediction that shifts toward user-generated dominance lower resilience.
Future work should test this association with denser time series and platform exposure measures that go beyond within-window post counts.

Several limitations motivate future extensions. The model treats media sources as homogeneous within each class; incorporating heterogeneity in credibility, audience size, or engagement could yield more realistic feedback. Psychological thresholds likely vary across groups \citep{Schwartz2013,Pfefferbaum2020}, and network structure can include homophily and community organization beyond ER or BA graphs \citep{Dandekar2013,McPherson2001}. Empirically, our arousal and risk labels rely on an LLM-based annotation pipeline; while we validate against human ratings on a subset, residual model-specific biases may affect absolute levels. More demanding early-warning tests also require substantially denser and more continuous time series.
