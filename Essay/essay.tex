\begin{abstract}
Information ecosystems can abruptly shift into polarized emotional states, yet prediction remains difficult because heterogeneous media feedback and individual psychological thresholds interact nonlinearly. We introduce a mixed-feedback model in which mainstream media provides stabilizing negative feedback and ``We-media'' provides amplifying positive feedback. Their competition is controlled by a single removal ratio that yields an analytic critical point $r_c$ and a Ginzburg-Landau potential that predicts pitchfork bifurcation and critical slowing down. Agent-based network simulations reproduce these theoretical signatures and map how thresholds, information density, media ecology, and local coupling shift the effective transition point. We then test empirical implications on large-scale Weibo data by operationalizing polarization $Q$, activity $a$, and a media-composition proxy $r_{\text{proxy}}$. The activity-to-jump link (H1) is supported in the pooled dataset, while the media-volatility prediction (H2) is supported in batch3 but weakens after controlling for sampling density, indicating an important confound. Early-warning signals based on critical slowing down (H4) are inconclusive under current data continuity and event sparsity, providing a boundary on empirical observability of the theoretical signature.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} phase transitions, collective emotion, mixed-feedback, emotional bubbles, critical slowing down, COVID-19

\section{Introduction}
Collective emotions can remain stable for long periods and then shift abruptly. Many computational models explain gradual drift, yet they struggle to account for system-wide transitions driven by nonlinear feedback and networked interactions \citep{Centola2010,Watts2002,Becker2017,Hasell2021,Chong2018,Lerman2024,Helfmann2023,Giacomo2025,Piao2023}. Online information ecosystems intensify this challenge: algorithmic amplification and adaptive exposure can push public sentiment toward tipping points.

The COVID-19 pandemic provided a global-scale setting in which public emotion evolved alongside an ``infodemic'' of heterogeneous risk information \citep{Green2020,Bavel2020,Wang2022,Aslam2020,Chou2020,Liu2024}. In such ecosystems, different media channels can exert opposing influences: mainstream media may dampen extremes, whereas attention-driven user-generated accounts (``We-media'') can amplify high-arousal narratives. Risk amplification frameworks \citep{Kasperson1988} and studies of moral outrage and emotional contagion \citep{Goldenberg2021,Brady2023,Crockett2017,Kramer2014,Fan2014} describe related mechanisms, but most formal models treat the information environment as a single feedback channel and thus struggle to explain abrupt regime shifts under mixed influence. We refer to these shifts as emotional bubbles---polarized states that appear stable until a critical threshold is crossed.

Motivated by this mixed environment, we model collective emotion as a competition between two feedback channels acting on individual arousal. At each update, individuals sample a small set of risk cues from the information environment, which we represent as a weighted mixture of mainstream media and We-media signals. Individuals summarize these cues into a perceived risk level and update to low, medium, or high arousal depending on whether perceived risk falls below a low threshold or exceeds a high threshold, reflecting heterogeneous psychological thresholds \citep{Granovetter1978}. The key modeling assumption is that the two channels respond differently to the current collective state: mainstream media tends to counteract extreme polarization (stabilizing negative feedback), whereas We-media tends to echo and reinforce the prevailing high-arousal direction (amplifying positive feedback). A single control parameter $r$ (the mainstream removal ratio) tunes the balance between stabilizing and amplifying influences and thus how close the system operates to instability.

We derive analytic predictions---a closed-form critical point $r_c$, a Ginzburg--Landau effective potential with pitchfork bifurcation, and critical slowing down---for when a mixed-feedback ecosystem becomes bistable and polarized. We validate these signatures in agent-based simulations on sparse networks, using finite-size diagnostics and systematic sweeps over thresholds, information density, media ecology, and local coupling. Finally, we translate the model into empirical proxies of polarization $Q$ (net emotional imbalance), activity $a$ (overall emotional engagement), and media composition $r_{\text{proxy}}$ (We-media share among media sources) from annotated Weibo posts (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}), providing complementary evidence for the activity--jump association (H1) and reporting media--volatility patterns (H2) as secondary and sensitive to sampling density. Our goal is not crisis-specific fitting, but a mechanism-based framework for when and why collective emotion becomes fragile.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\subsection{Mixed-feedback model framework}
We consider a population of $N$ individuals with three arousal states: high ($H$), medium ($M$), and low ($L$). Let $(\rho_H,\rho_M,\rho_L)$ denote the population fractions with $\rho_H+\rho_M+\rho_L=1$. We introduce two orthogonal macroscopic variables: the polarization direction
\begin{equation}
q(t)=\rho_H(t)-\rho_L(t),
\label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
and the system activity
\begin{equation}
a(t)=\rho_H(t)+\rho_L(t)=1-\rho_M(t).
\label{eq:a}
\end{equation}
These order parameters decouple symmetry breaking from the depletion of the moderate buffer, with $\rho_H=(a+q)/2$ and $\rho_L=(a-q)/2$.

At each update, an individual samples $k$ risk signals from the environment, each labeled ``risk'' with probability $p_i$. Let $S\sim\text{Binomial}(k,p_i)$ denote the number of risk signals and $\hat{p}=S/k$ the perceived risk fraction. Individuals transition to $H$ if $\hat{p}\ge\phi$, to $L$ if $\hat{p}\le\theta$, and otherwise remain in $M$, where $\theta<\phi$ define low and high arousal thresholds.

The information environment is modeled as a competition between mainstream media (negative feedback) and We-media (positive feedback). The mainstream signal is
\begin{equation}
p^{\text{main}}(q)=\frac{1-q}{2},
\label{eq:pmain}
\end{equation}
which counteracts the current polarization. For We-media we use two regimes. In the symmetric validation regime, used for analytical verification, we set
\begin{equation}
p^{\text{we}}(q)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{q}{2},
\label{eq:pwe_sym}
\end{equation}
which enforces $q\to -q$ symmetry. In the asymmetric, realistic regime we allow activity coupling via
\begin{equation}
p^{\text{we}}(q,a)=\rho_H=\frac{a+q}{2}.
\label{eq:pwe_asym}
\end{equation}
The overall environmental risk is the weighted average
\begin{equation}
p_{\text{env}}(q,a;r)=\frac{(1-r)n_m p^{\text{main}} + r n_w p^{\text{we}}}{(1-r)n_m + r n_w}, \qquad r\in[0,1],
\label{eq:penv}
\end{equation}
where $n_m$ and $n_w$ are the baseline strengths of mainstream and We-media sources, and $r$ is the mainstream removal ratio.

\subsection{Mean-field analysis and critical point}
Under the symmetric regime, $q=0$ is a fixed point with $p_{\text{env}}=1/2$. Linearizing the mean-field dynamics around $q=0$ yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{dq}{dt}=-q+\mathcal{S}(p),
\label{eq:q_dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}(p)$ is the collective response. We define the psychological sensitivity
\begin{equation}
\chi=\left.\frac{d\mathcal{S}}{dp}\right|_{p=1/2},
\label{eq:chi}
\end{equation}
and the feedback gradient $\Gamma=\left.\partial p_{\text{env}}/\partial q\right|_{q=0}$. The stability condition $\chi\Gamma=1$ gives the analytic critical point
\begin{equation}
r_c=\frac{n_m(\chi+2)}{n_m(\chi+2)+n_w(\chi-2)}.
\label{eq:rc}
\end{equation}
Near $r\approx r_c$, the order parameter obeys a Ginzburg-Landau form,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dq}{dt}=-\frac{\partial V_{\text{eff}}(q)}{\partial q}+\eta(t),\qquad V_{\text{eff}}(q)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha q^2+\frac{u}{4}q^4,
\label{eq:gl}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha\propto(r_c-r)$ and $u>0$, predicting a pitchfork bifurcation and critical slowing down, $\tau\sim 1/|r_c-r|$.

\subsection{Agent-based network simulations}
We simulate microscopic dynamics on ER and BA networks with size $N$ and average degree $\langle k\rangle$. Updates are asynchronous: each step updates a fraction of nodes, and we report time in both steps and sweeps. Local coupling $\beta$ mixes the global signal $p_{\text{env}}$ with neighbor information, allowing us to quantify how topology and local reinforcement shift the effective transition. We denote the finite-size network polarization by $Q$, which corresponds to the mean-field order parameter $q$. In the symmetric regime, trajectories select either polarized branch with equal probability, so the signed mean $\langle Q\rangle$ cancels; we therefore measure $|Q|$ or align signs across runs (by flipping trajectories to a common branch) before averaging. We estimate finite-size transition points using Binder cumulant crossings,
\begin{equation}
U_4(r;N)=1-\frac{\langle Q^4\rangle}{3\langle Q^2\rangle^2}.
\label{eq:binder}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Empirical data and operational proxies}
Empirical validation uses a Weibo corpus spanning January 2019 to July 2025, covering posts from mainstream media, government accounts, We-media, and public users. Across all crawls we collected 963,249 records; 935,005 contain a valid post identifier (\texttt{mid}), corresponding to 880,100 unique posts after de-duplication. The hypothesis tests reported in this paper use a labeled subset of 90,982 posts (All=master+batch3). Each post is labeled for emotional arousal and risk content using a DistilBERT-based classifier \citep{Sanh2020}.

We aggregate labeled posts into 4-hour (4H) time windows and compute window-level arousal fractions $(X_H,X_M,X_L)$ and media-type counts. From these windows we construct polarization $Q=X_H-X_L$ and activity $a=X_H+X_L$. Media composition is proxied by
\begin{equation}
r_{\text{proxy}}=\frac{n_{\text{wemedia}}}{n_{\text{wemedia}}+n_{\text{mainstream}}+n_{\text{government}}},
\label{eq:rproxy}
\end{equation}
treating government accounts as part of the official narrative channel. For statistical testing, we group consecutive 4H windows into weekly segments and use segments with sufficient valid windows as analysis units (All: 120 segments for H1; batch3: 114 segments for H2). H1 links activity to jump intensity using the segment-level 95th percentile of $|d|Q|/dt|$. H2 links $r_{\text{proxy}}$ to volatility defined as $\text{std}(Q)$ within segments. H4 probes critical slowing down using rolling $AC1(|Q|)$ and $\mathrm{Var}(|Q|)$ before jump events with block-aware placebo tests.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Theoretical bifurcation and critical point}
In the symmetric validation regime, the model exhibits a pitchfork bifurcation as the control parameter $r$ crosses the analytic threshold $r_c$. For the baseline configuration $n_m=10$, $n_w=5$, $\phi=0.54$, $\theta=0.46$, and $k=50$, the theory predicts $r_c\approx 0.753$. The bifurcation structure is captured by the order parameter $q$ and is explained by the effective potential $V_{\text{eff}}(q)$, which transitions from a single well to a double well as $r$ increases (Fig.~\ref{fig:theory}). This yields a mechanistic interpretation of emotional bubbles: as negative feedback weakens, the system becomes bistable and small perturbations select one polarized branch. Having established this analytic baseline, we next test whether it survives networked interactions and finite-size noise.

\subsection{Network simulation validation and finite-size stability}
We then simulate agent-based dynamics on ER and BA networks to evaluate robustness beyond the mean-field limit. In the symmetric regime, branch selection is random, so the signed mean $\langle Q\rangle$ cancels; using $|Q|$ or aligned signed $Q$ recovers the expected bifurcation. Finite-size scaling further supports the transition when estimated via Binder cumulant crossings, which are more stable than susceptibility peaks. Figure~\ref{fig:network} shows that the bifurcation persists under networked interactions and that finite-size diagnostics localize the critical region. When symmetry is relaxed or local coupling is added, the effective transition point shifts, indicating structural effects rather than implementation artifacts. This static validation sets the stage for testing the dynamic signature predicted by theory.

\subsection{Critical slowing down and time-scale alignment}
Theory predicts a dynamic signature near criticality: as $r$ approaches $r_c$, the restoring force vanishes and relaxation times diverge. To test this prediction, we align time scales between asynchronous ABM updates and continuous-time theory by converting steps to sweeps, then evaluate autocorrelation and variance-based indicators. The simulations show increasing relaxation time and rising autocorrelation as $r$ approaches $r_c$, consistent with $\tau\sim 1/|r_c-r|$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:csd}). This alignment is essential for interpreting early-warning signals across modeling layers.

\subsection{Parameter landscapes and structural effects}
The critical boundary depends on psychological thresholds, information density, media ecology, and local coupling, so we map how each factor shifts the effective transition. The sensitivity $\chi$ and the critical point $r_c$ vary systematically across $(\phi,\theta)$, and a transition exists only when $\chi>2$. Increasing information density $k$ generally raises $\chi$ and shifts $r_c$, while a higher We-media ratio lowers the effective transition point, indicating greater fragility. Local coupling $\beta$ re-parameterizes the macroscopic boundary by amplifying neighborhood reinforcement. These effects are consistent across mean-field calculations and network simulations (Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}), showing that topology and media ecology jointly determine how close the system operates to criticality. With these mechanisms established, we next confront the theoretical signatures with empirical data.

\subsection{Empirical validation of theoretical signatures}
We operationalize polarization $Q$, activity $a$, and media composition $r_{\text{proxy}}$ from the 4H-aggregated labeled corpus and evaluate hypotheses at the segment level. Consistent with the theoretical prediction that higher activity depletes moderates and increases susceptibility, H1 is supported in the pooled dataset (All=master+batch3): segments with higher activity show larger jump intensity (95th percentile of $|d|Q|/dt|$; Pearson $r=0.241$, $p=0.00798$; Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical}a).

For H2, $r_{\text{proxy}}$ measures We-media dominance (higher $r_{\text{proxy}}$ implies weaker official negative feedback). In batch3, higher $r_{\text{proxy}}$ correlates with higher volatility of $Q$ (Pearson $r=0.265$, $p=0.00434$; Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical}b). However, controlling for segment-level sampling density substantially attenuates the effect (partial $r=0.078$, $p=0.411$), indicating an important confound.

Early-warning signals predicted by critical slowing down (H4) are inconclusive in the current data: event-aligned $AC1(|Q|)$ and $\mathrm{Var}(|Q|)$ do not exceed block-aware placebo baselines (Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical}c), and finer aggregation (1H/2H) breaks continuity and yields no eligible events (Appendix).

\section{Discussion}
This study develops a mixed-feedback theory of collective emotion that links competing media dynamics to phase-transition behavior. By combining thresholded arousal updating with antagonistic feedback channels, the model yields an analytic critical point $r_c$ and an effective potential that interprets emotional bubbles as a loss of stabilizing feedback that creates bistability. Beyond static bifurcation, the theory predicts critical slowing down as a dynamical signature near instability. Agent-based simulations reproduce these signatures under networked interactions, diagnose finite-size effects with Binder cumulants, and map how thresholds, information density, media ecology, and local coupling shift the effective transition boundary.

Empirical analyses provide targeted support. In the pooled dataset, higher activity predicts larger jump intensity (H1), consistent with the mechanism that depletion of moderates increases susceptibility to abrupt shifts. In batch3, greater We-media dominance (higher $r_{\text{proxy}}$) is associated with higher volatility (H2), but the effect weakens after controlling for segment-level sampling density, indicating an important confound. Early-warning signals based on critical slowing down (H4) remain inconclusive under current data continuity and event sparsity, delineating a practical boundary on detectability in public social media traces.

Several limitations motivate future extensions. The model treats media sources as homogeneous within each class; incorporating heterogeneity in credibility, audience size, or engagement could yield more realistic feedback. Psychological thresholds likely vary across groups \citep{Schwartz2013,Pfefferbaum2020}, and network structure can include homophily and community organization beyond ER or BA graphs \citep{Bisgin2012,Dandekar2013,McPherson2001}. Empirically, denser and more continuous time series, alongside designs that disentangle media composition from attention and sampling density, will be necessary to move from correlational evidence toward stronger causal tests and to evaluate early-warning signals in practice.

\section*{Appendix: Empirical Robustness and Data Continuity}
To make the H1 and H2 tests comparable across datasets, we compute segment-level statistics from 4H-aggregated time series. Jump intensity is defined as the 95th percentile of $|d|Q|/dt|$ within each segment, and volatility is defined as $\mathrm{std}(Q)$ within the same segment. The density confound in H2 is assessed by controlling for the number of valid windows per segment, which attenuates the raw correlation between $r_{\text{proxy}}$ and volatility (Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical}b and Table~\ref{tab:empirical}).

To evaluate H4, we adopt a block-aware event study that only uses continuous segments for rolling $AC1(|Q|)$ and $\mathrm{Var}(|Q|)$, and we compare real events to placebo events sampled within the same continuity blocks. This design guards against artifacts from gaps but reduces the number of eligible events in sparse series. To reduce potential over-smoothing at 4H aggregation, we tested 2H and 1H resolutions while keeping comparable window durations (e.g., $\sim$48h rolling and $\sim$96h pre-event lookback). Finer aggregation amplifies missingness and shortens continuous blocks, which breaks rolling-window assumptions and yields eligible=0/events=0 under strict block-aware evaluation. This sensitivity indicates that, despite a clear theoretical prediction, empirical detection of critical slowing down in social media data requires substantially denser and more continuous sampling.
